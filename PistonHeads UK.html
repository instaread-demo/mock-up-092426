2026 Peugeot E-208 GTI | PH Review

Another month, another electric hot hatch - can the E-208 possibly live up to its 205 GTI design nods?

By Stephen Dobie / Thursday 24 September 2026 / 34 comments

Listen to audio version of this article
Sponsored by
0:00 / 6:27
Instaread

Here we go, then: another electric hot hatchback. This one lands with surely the biggest weight on its shoulders, the retro font and C-pillar location of its GTI badges the most on-the-nose 205 callback we’ve seen applied to any recent performance Peugeot.

Can you blame them? Selling a fully plug-in GTI to an enthusiast buyer won’t be a stroll along the Seine for any dealer, thus the 205 GTI references are ladled generously upon this E-208 GTI. There’s the red stripes adorning its grille, headlamps and wheelarch extensions. The natty patterned fabric of its seats. The searingly scarlet mats and carpets. 

Thankfully, the form is all backed up by function. Wider tracks over a standard 208 and naughtier negative camber give it a pugnacious stance. Those sports seats are impressively low-slung for a front-driven supermini and hug you nicely on a challenging road. And while the carpets are destined to end up sullied by mud, they bring genuine boldness to the interior. The fundamental ingredients are all as per the Stellantis electric hot hatch recipe. A 280hp motor drives the front axle with the help of a Torsen mechanical diff. Massive 355mm Alcon brakes slow you down, with the regen decoupled in Sport mode (though optionally turned back on). The suspension is 25mm lower, notably stiffer and complemented by hydraulic bump stops. 

All of its hardware matches that of the Vauxhall Corsa GSE, in fact, though there’s a new steering tune here to better harmonise with the dinky wheel of Peugeot’s love-or-loathe i-Cockpit. Both cars feature augmented sound, the electronic warble here tangibly different from the more traditional, combustion-edged tone of the GSE.

Why so many shared bits? This GTI was never in the plan. Alain Favey landed the Peugeot CEO role in February 2025 and rubber-stamped this car shortly after, reversing years of reticence by his predecessor. Elements of this car betray its fast-track to production, then, and the engineers decided to direct their limited time and budget at improved battery cooling – to ensure strong performance across its state of charge during quick driving – rather than dabble in the world of virtual gears, which are absent here. Albeit not ruled out for any potential successors.

First impressions can’t help but recall the Corsa. This 208 is a tautly sprung thing through town and you can feel its 1.5 tonnes being kept mercilessly in check. With speed, its damping seems to relax a little, the GTI breathing a little better with the road than the GSE, and it’s very easy to build a rapid flow. Boy, is it quick, feeling good for its 5.5-second 0-62mph claim yet possessing nicely linear throttle response. 

The steering is unfailingly eager, and a nice reminder of the setup in 208 GTIs of old – I owned and mostly loved my Peugeot Sport edition – though the rack feels at its best in the Normal drive parameter, Sport adding far too much sticky, artificial weight. It’s a shame you can’t mix and match settings like in a Cupra Raval (or any other Volkswagen Group equivalent); Sport also houses a loosened stability control setup, though regardless of your drive mode, its little orange light seems to flash vigorously as you exit corners, no matter how precisely you’ve turned in or prudently powered out. It can’t be turned off.

Which does mean this GTI lacks the playful edge that might make it an authentic 205 tribute act. This is a car that’s composed across a tricky, undulating road and which rewards you for the commitment you throw at it. But combine its absence of a gearbox – virtual or otherwise – with its fairly locked-down cornering attitude and there’s only so much depth the experience can offer. 

Still, the engineers’ efficiency work – apparently trickled down from the 9X8 Hypercar that’s been a mainstay of the World Endurance Championship (and thus Le Mans) for four years – can surely take some of the credit for the respectable 2.7 miles/kWh we scored in reasonably keen driving. Enough for a comfortable 120-mile range if you’re confident of a charger at the end of it. 

Nope, that’s not game-changing. But nor are the equivalent claims of its rivals, and there’s a mite more usability than a number of those in here. Adults can squeeze in the back, the boot is a decent 309 litres, and the interior has a general air of quality about it, neatly mixing premium and pragmatic materials to keep the price competitive. Sure, the climate control lives within a sub-menu of the touchscreen, and it’s yet another modern car whose core functions prove fiddly to conduct on the move. But the ADAS can be extinguished with a long button press and the seats are wonderfully supportive. Those in a good hot hatchback always are.

You’ll pay £33,495 after the Electric Car Grant (£500 more than the Corsa), or £349 a month with Peugeot’s initial lease offer (which commands a chunky deposit). That buys an almost fully stacked E-208 GTI, your key options being a pano roof (£500), any colour beyond standard Okenite White (£750-£950) or a set of Hankook Ventus S1 Evo3 tyres (no cost) which claim 233 miles of WLTP range rather than the 219 miles of its standard Michelin Pilot Sport 4 S. Perhaps they’ll unlock a mite more movement in the chassis, too.

While it’s a car that feels precisely the sum of its parts, we remain eager to spend more time in it. This is an impressively booming sector with the E-208 GTI joining efforts from (deep breath) Abarth, Alfa Romeo, Alpine, Cupra, Mini, Vauxhall and soon Volkswagen. While we’ve not yet sampled an electric hot hatch that can fully replicate the engagement of its forebears, nothing in this market wants for charm or design chutzpah. Its exquisite stance, confident handling and relative practicality place this Peugeot right in the mix if you can live with its range. What we wouldn’t give for a lighter, lither E-208 Rallye with a few hundred kilos less to hustle, though…
