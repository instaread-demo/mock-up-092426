A Ram 1500's frame may be bent, but not from a crash. Instead, it can happen from wrong towing.

Ted Gibbons, a Torque News reader, photographed the truck on setup day at a fair in Wynantskill, NY, and he didn't hesitate on what caused it: "The person bent the frame of this Dodge 1500 towing too much weight."

A Second Case in Five Days

Torque News reported on a different Ram 1500 five days earlier, stuck on the side of a road in Ontario after reportedly towing a 12-ton trailer, nearly double the truck's own 12,750-pound max rating.

That truck got stuck. This one may be worse.

A stuck truck can get recovered and driven home, but a bent frame can total the vehicle outright.


What We Can and Can't Confirm

Torque News hasn't independently inspected this truck. Gibbons' claim is an eyewitness account from setup day at the fair, not a verified mechanical finding.

What's visible in the photo is a dark gray Ram 1500 hitched to a large black enclosed cargo trailer, the kind commonly used to haul tents, staging, and vendor equipment. That's consistent with fairground setup work, heavy, awkwardly distributed cargo loaded without much thought given to how it sits on the axle.

How a Frame Actually Bends

Advertising


Frame damage from towing isn't random. It's almost always excessive tongue weight, the downward force the trailer exerts on the hitch, often made dramatically worse the instant the truck hits a bump, dip, or pothole while under load.

One Ford F-150 owner's frame bent directly behind the suspension mount after hitting an unmarked speed bump while towing a loaded dump trailer. Forum estimates put the momentary tongue weight over 10,000 pounds at the moment of impact, static weight multiplied by the leverage of the bump.

That's the mechanism worth understanding here. A truck can drive fine for miles under an overloaded trailer, right up until one bad bump turns static overload into a permanent bend.

This Isn't a Ram Defect

Say this plainly: a frame bending under a load that exceeds its rating is the frame doing exactly what an overloaded frame does. Any half-ton truck, from any manufacturer, faces the same physics.

That distinction matters because it got raised in the comments on our own Ontario story. One commenter wrote that "rams have had notorious problems with the frames being weak exactly where this one damaged," while another pushed back immediately: "Just because the trailer is a 12 ton trailer... doesn't mean it weighs 12 tons... however it's rated to carry 12 tons including its own weight."

Both readers were circling the same real issue without naming it directly: the frame's rating, not the frame's manufacturer, is what determines whether it survives the load.

Half-Ton Payload Gets Eaten Fast

Torque News has documented this exact math on other Ram 1500s. A 7,500-pound travel trailer at a standard 12-15% tongue weight puts 900 to 1,125 pounds directly on the hitch, and a half-ton's door-jamb payload rating is often only 1,350 pounds to begin with.

Add a driver, a passenger, and gear in the bed, and there's often only a couple hundred pounds of real margin left before the truck is over its rating, regardless of what the trailer's own tow rating says. A Toyota Tundra owner learned this the hard way towing a trailer that looked legal by tow rating but put the combination near or over GVWR once it hit a certified scale.

The Real Cost of Getting This Wrong

Frame repair costs scale with severity. Straightening runs $1,500 to $5,000. Welding repairs run $500 to $2,500. A full frame replacement runs $15,000 to $30,000, often enough to total a truck and leave it with a salvage title.

Compare that to the Ontario truck, which got winched out, driven home, and back on the road. A bent frame is a permanently different kind of problem.

What This Actually Teaches Every Truck Owner

Although we have two RAM1500s with two different failure modes, the shared root cause is one. It looks like, nobody weighed the load before hitching up. That's not a brand problem. It's a habit problem, and it's one every half-ton owner towing this fall should take seriously.

A CAT scale ticket costs less than a fast-food meal. A bent frame costs a truck.

Have You Weighed Your Load Before Towing?

If you tow regularly, have you ever gotten a loaded trailer weighed at a CAT scale before hitching up, or do you trust the trailer's stated weight rating?

And after seeing two Ram 1500s run into trouble in the same week, would that change how carefully you'd load a trailer for your own truck, regardless of the badge on the tailgate?
